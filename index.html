A Study on the Impact of
Online Reviews on Consumer
Purchase Decisions

An empirical inquiry into how review valence, reviewer expertise, and content–product fit shape trust and buying behaviour in digital marketplaces.

02 / 12 — At a Glance

Abstract in Numbers

A validated scale measuring review credibility, informativeness, and persuasive power was tested on 224 e‑commerce shoppers to isolate what actually moves purchase intention.

03 / 12 — Objectives

What the Study Set Out to Do

  • 01 Develop a psychometrically sound measurement scale for online review influence.
  • 02 Examine the differential effects of review valence, reviewer expertise, and content–product fit on purchase intention.
  • 03 Quantify the relative contribution of perceived credibility versus informativeness in shaping consumer trust.
  • 04 Deliver actionable, evidence‑based recommendations for e‑commerce platforms on review presentation.
04 / 12 — Grounded in Prior Work

Literature Foundations

Chevalier & Mayzlin (2006)
Positive reviews lift sales; negative reviews suppress intention even more strongly.
Ghose & Ipeirotis (2010)
Textual quality, readability, and linguistic accuracy drive trust beyond star ratings.
Filieri (2016)
Review extremity, content, and platform reputation shape perceived trustworthiness.
Mayzlin, Dover & Chevalier (2014)
Manipulated or fake reviews erode consumer trust and distort purchasing behaviour.

Across two decades of research, one thread holds: credibility cues consistently outweigh raw star ratings in shaping the final purchase decision.

05 / 12 — Methodology

Empirical & Descriptive Design

Structured questionnaires on a five‑point Likert scale were administered via convenience sampling, supplemented by peer‑reviewed and government secondary sources. Data was analysed in SPSS v21 using Chi‑square, ANOVA, and Pearson correlation.

06 / 12 — Result

Which Cue Wins Most Often?

Across six demographic lenses (age, gender, education, occupation, purchase frequency, platform), each group converged overwhelmingly on one factor as most influential.

Result: Visual proof — customer photos and videos — topped the greatest number of demographic breakdowns, ahead of verified‑buyer badges and star ratings.
07 / 12 — Result

Drivers of Purchase Intention

The validated scale isolates the independent contribution of four review dimensions to intention formation.

Result: Valence carries the largest single effect, but credibility — the composite measure — explains the most variance overall, confirming it as the master construct.
08 / 12 — Result

A Closer Look: Female Respondents

Peak convergence for female respondents across six separate survey statements — the most internally consistent demographic slice in the dataset.

Result: Agreement stayed in a tight 86–94% band across every statement — except one, where 86% actively disagreed reviews are an important information source, hinting at a trust/behaviour gap.
09 / 12 — Statistical Significance

Testing the Relationships

Observed test statistics against their critical thresholds at α = .05.

Result: χ²(12) = 64.6, p < .001 links age to review‑factor preference; F(1,52) = 25.3, p < .001 confirms age predicts overall attitude score — both comfortably clear their critical thresholds.
10 / 12 — Result

A Divided Sense of Trust

Weekly online shoppers versus low‑frequency, highly‑educated skeptics — two poles of the same dataset.

Result: Frequent shoppers converge fully on "high credibility / trust," while occasional, postgraduate‑educated respondents converge just as strongly on the opposite pole — trust is segmented, not universal.
11 / 12 — Discussion

Reading the Results Together

  • · Visual evidence — photos and videos — now rivals or beats text credibility as a trust signal, especially among private‑sector employed and platform‑engaged respondents.
  • · Trust is not uniform: frequent shoppers trust reviews readily, while highly‑educated occasional shoppers remain the most skeptical segment.
  • · Age significantly predicts overall attitude (F = 25.27, p < .001), meaning platforms may need age‑aware review presentation, not a one‑size‑fits‑all layout.
  • · Findings extend the Theory of Planned Behavior — reviewer expertise and content cues act as normative and control beliefs that reshape intention, echoing Fernandes et al. (2022).
12 / 12 — Closing

Limitations & Path Forward

A convenience sample of 224, concentrated geographically, limits generalisability; self‑report bias may inflate agreement rates. Future work should pursue longitudinal, multi‑state designs.

Conclusion: Platforms should surface reviewer‑expertise indicators and enforce content–product alignment — credibility, not volume, converts.
