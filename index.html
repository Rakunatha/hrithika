The Study

224 shoppers, one running question: what makes a review believable enough to act on?

Respondents worked through a short agreement scale covering how they read, weigh and act on reviews — then answered again broken out by age, gender, education, occupation and how often they shop online. The five findings ahead are the patterns that held up.

Who

224 shoppers across age, gender, education and occupation groups, reached in person and online.

How

A five-point agreement scale, from "strongly disagree" to "strongly agree," on statements about trust, credibility and habit.

What for

To see which review signal — star rating, verified badge, written detail or photo — actually earns a click to buy.

What catches the eye first

Result

Ask shoppers what single thing shapes their read on a review, and the answer splits hard by age. Some groups converge almost unanimously on one cue — a verified-buyer badge, a written account — while others are far more evenly torn between star ratings and photos.

Discussion

There's no universal "trust cue." A review layout built for a 20-year-old and one built for a 45-year-old should probably not look the same — the badge that reassures one age group barely registers with another.

Do reviews actually help decide?

Result

Nearly 90% of women agreed reviews help them make an informed purchase decision. Men leaned positive too — split about 60/40 between "agree" and "strongly agree" — but with less of the outright conviction seen among women.

Discussion

Both groups are already convinced, so the open opportunity isn't proving reviews matter — it's making the strongest, most decisive language land with the segment still sitting in the middle.

Figure 03

Are reviews a real source of information?

Result

Postgraduate respondents were unanimous — every one of them called reviews an important source of product information. Undergraduates ran the other way, tilting toward "disagree." Other education bands landed somewhere in between.

Discussion

Comfort reading dense, comparative information appears to track with education level. Simpler, more visual review formats — photos, short verdicts — may do more for the segment that isn't yet convinced reviews are worth the read.

Figure 04

How much do shoppers trust the score?

Result

Around 86% of women rated review credibility "high." Men were far more divided — roughly 60% called it "very low," the rest "high" — with almost nobody landing in the middle.

Discussion

Credibility isn't a fixed property of a review — it's a perception gap between audiences. A visible proof point (purchase verification, photo evidence) does more work for the more skeptical half of the room.

Figure 05

Trust has limits

Result

Close to 90% of women say they only "trust a little," rather than fully. Men split almost evenly between "do not trust at all" and "trust" outright — hardly anyone chose neutral.

Discussion

Even shoppers who read and value reviews rarely hand over full trust. Reading and believing are two different steps — which is why credibility signals, not just review volume, are what actually move a decision.

Takeaway — Reviews already work. The next gain isn't more of them; it's sharper trust signals aimed at the specific shoppers still sitting on the fence.
