RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Influencer Liability for Misleading Advertisements

A Tort Law Analysis

Research Presentation • Hrithika Suri • Empirical Study • n = 220

RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Research Focus

What the study examines

  • Influencer marketing has become a large-scale commercial communication channel.
  • The study examines tort-law liability for misleading influencer advertisements.
  • It considers awareness, perceptions of responsibility, disclosure, consumer harm and regulatory responses.
  • The analysis combines legal doctrine, consumer-protection theory and empirical survey evidence.
RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Study Design

Methodology at a glance

  • Empirical research design using a structured questionnaire.
  • Sample: 220 internet users who had encountered branded content on social platforms.
  • Convenience sampling across age, gender, education, area and occupation.
  • Five-point Likert scale used to capture attitudes and perceptions.
  • Data processed using SPSS 21; the paper reports chi-square, ANOVA and Pearson correlation analyses.
RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Key Finding 01

Young adults show strong support for influencer accountability

  • 68% of respondents aged 18–24 strongly agreed that influencers should be legally accountable for deceptive posts.
  • 22% agreed and 10% remained neutral.
  • Result: a clear majority in the youngest group favoured legal accountability.
  • Discussion: the paper links this to generational sensitivity to deceptive digital marketing and the case for stronger consumer protection.
RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Key Finding 02

Disclosure is perceived as inadequate

  • 55% of female participants perceived current disclosure practices as insufficient.
  • 38% of male participants held the same view; 7% were undecided.
  • Result: inadequate disclosure is a prominent consumer concern.
  • Discussion: the paper recommends gender-responsive education and stronger transparency mechanisms to reduce information asymmetry.
RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Key Finding 03

Legal awareness varies with educational attainment

  • 62% of respondents with a bachelor’s degree indicated high awareness of tort liability.
  • Only 41% of respondents with high-school education demonstrated comparable awareness.
  • Result: the study identifies a notable legal-literacy gap.
  • Discussion: the paper suggests integrating basic tort and consumer-rights concepts into secondary education and outreach.
RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Key Finding 04

Marketing professionals encounter misleading content frequently

  • 71% of professionals in marketing reported encountering misleading influencer content weekly.
  • The comparable figures were 49% for students and 30% for retirees.
  • Result: exposure differs substantially across occupational groups.
  • Discussion: the paper calls for industry-specific guidelines and clearer standards for influencer collaborations.
RESEARCH PRESENTATION

Key Finding 05

Urban respondents favour stronger regulatory penalties

  • 74% of respondents from urban areas believed regulatory agencies should impose stricter penalties.
  • 18% favoured platform self-regulation and 8% were uncertain.
  • Result: stronger formal enforcement was the dominant preference.
  • Discussion: the paper connects this to greater exposure to digital advertising ecosystems and argues for robust enforcement mechanisms.

Overall Results

What the five graphs collectively indicate

  • Awareness and accountability concerns are substantial across the sample.
  • Disclosure and transparency emerge as recurring regulatory concerns.
  • Education is associated with differences in reported legal awareness.
  • Occupational exposure and geographic context shape perceptions of influencer-related risks.
  • The paper reports broad support for stronger, more structured regulatory interventions.

Legal & Policy Implications

From empirical findings to tort-law reform

  • Codify clear statutory disclosure duties and uniform sponsored-content labels.
  • Develop stronger compliance and monitoring mechanisms for influencer advertising.
  • Promote automated detection and user-reporting tools for undisclosed promotional material.
  • Expand consumer education on documentation, rights and legal remedies.
  • Encourage contractual risk assessment, liability and compliance clauses for brands and influencers.

Limitations & Future Research

Read the results within the study's limits

  • The sample is a convenience sample of 220 internet users and may not represent the wider population.
  • Urban and highly educated respondents may be over-represented.
  • Self-report measures may involve recall and social-desirability bias.
  • Future research should use longitudinal designs, stratified random sampling and qualitative methods.
  • The paper also recommends studying influencer liability alongside consumer-protection, advertising and privacy frameworks.

Conclusion

A clearer legal architecture for influencer marketing

  • The study identifies significant concern about misleading influencer advertising and a strong appetite for clearer accountability.
  • Its central policy direction is greater transparency, enforceable disclosure duties and stronger institutional oversight.
  • The empirical findings support continued alignment of tort-law principles with contemporary digital marketing practices.
  • Future work should broaden the evidence base and test these conclusions with more representative samples.
